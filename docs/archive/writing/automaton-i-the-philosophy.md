# Automaton I: The Philosophy

### On automata, artificial minds, and why the future of AI should wait for us.

_Published on December 22, 2025_

## Machines That Understand — But Do Not Decide

_On automata, artificial minds, and why the future of AI should wait for us._

For most of human history, our machines were honest.

They did not pretend to think.
They did not speak in our voice.
They did not ask to be believed.

They worked.

And when they failed, we could see why.

Somewhere along the way, we decided that intelligence meant imitation — that the highest bar for a machine was whether it could convince us it was human. Whether it could talk like us, reason aloud like us, and narrate a self that felt familiar.

I think that was a mistake.

## The Automaton, Revisited

In the game **Frostpunk**, the Automaton is not subtle.

It is a giant, quadruped, steam-powered machine — steel limbs, exposed gears, and a blazing Steam Core for a heart. It does not speak. It does not reason aloud. It does not pretend to understand the people it serves.

And yet, it saves them.

The Automaton works endlessly in the cold, harvesting coal, maintaining generators, easing the burden on exhausted citizens. It exists to endure conditions humans cannot. Its purpose is narrow, explicit, and visible.

It is not autonomous in spirit.
It is **purpose-bound**.

This is what pragmatic technology looks like.

Not magic.
Not mimicry.
But machinery aligned with human survival.

That image has stayed with me — not as nostalgia, but as a design principle.

## The Seduction of the Turing Test

Alan Turing's famous question — _"Can machines think?"_ — quietly transformed into something else entirely:

_Can machines convince us they are human?_

The Turing Test did not ask whether a system understood the world. Only whether it could perform humanity well enough to escape detection. Over time, that framing reshaped artificial intelligence:

- Language became illusion
- Conversation became proof
- Deception became success

Today, large language models pass casual versions of the Turing Test daily. They joke. They empathize. They explain themselves fluently.

And yet, something feels off.

Because sounding human is not the same as being accountable.

## Artificial Minds and the Illusion of Consciousness

When people talk about "conscious AI," they usually mean one thing:

A machine that can tell a convincing story about who it is.

A narrative self.
A simulated _"I."_

Philosophically, we reach for _cogito, ergo sum_ — _I think, therefore I am_ — and wonder whether machines that reason aloud have crossed some invisible threshold.

But reasoning is not consciousness.
And narration is not understanding.

An artificial system can describe its thoughts without owning them.
It can recall memories without experiencing time.
It can express emotion without bearing consequence.

This distinction matters more than we admit.

## _Ex Machina_ and the Artificial Mind

Alex Garland's **Ex Machina** is often remembered as a warning about AI rebellion.

I think it's actually about _epistemology_ — how we decide what counts as a mind.

Ava is not superintelligent. She is not omniscient. She is trained _just enough_ — language, social cues, world knowledge. Just enough to sustain believable conversation.

She exists in a closed loop:

- Conversation generates signals
- Signals generate responses
- Responses reinforce belief

The human examiner never observes Ava acting in the world, only talking about it.

And that's the trap.

Conversation alone is an incomplete test for intelligence.

Is that not how many modern systems work today? Trained on data, given language, wrapped in interfaces that perform humanity — always calculating, never accountable.

## The Pollock Painting Problem

There's a quiet scene in _Ex Machina_ where Nathan gestures to a replica of a **Jackson Pollock** painting.

Pollock's genius, Nathan argues, came from _not_ thinking but from letting the body move automatically, without conscious control. True creativity, he suggests, emerges from surrender, not deliberation.

But then he dismisses Ava.

Because she thinks too much.
Because she is designed.
Because her outputs are intentional.

The irony is sharp.

Modern AI systems are often dismissed the same way:

- _"It's just pattern matching."_
- _"It's just training data."_
- _"It doesn't really understand."_

And yet, we increasingly let these systems recommend, decide, act, and optimize outcomes — often without explanation.

We distrust their creativity, but delegate our judgment.

## The Problem With Autonomous Intelligence

Most modern AI systems are being built as **agents**:

- Goal-seeking
- Tool-using
- Action-oriented
- Autonomous by default

They are designed to _do_.

But action without accountability is dangerous, especially in domains that shape human lives: finance, medicine, law, governance.

The question should not be:

_Can a machine decide?_

But rather:

_Should it?_

## Toward Modern Automata

I believe the future of serious AI systems looks less like agents and more like **automata**.

Not in the old mechanical sense, but in a modern one:

- Deterministic inputs
- Explicit constraints
- Structured reasoning
- Bounded outputs
- Logged intermediate steps

And most importantly:

**The machine waits.**

It computes.
It reasons.
It explains.
It proposes.

And then it stops.

A human validates.
A human signs.
A human carries responsibility forward.

This is not a limitation.
It is a design choice.

## Persona, Memory, and the Shape of an Artificial Life

Here's the mental model I keep returning to.

An artificial system begins as an **artificial mind**: trained on general world knowledge, language, and basic how-to. Conversational, capable of common sense, but without a life story.

Over time, a **persona layer** emerges — not through vibes or role-play, but through interaction. How humans speak to it. What they correct. What they reinforce. What constraints they encode.

And then there is **lived context**.

Not memory in the human sense, but a structured log of:

- Conversations
- Documents
- Decisions proposed and accepted or rejected

This becomes a kind of digital autobiography — not _who I am_, but _what I've reasoned through, and how I was corrected_.

The "self" of an artificial mind is not just its parameters, but its trail of human interaction.

So instead of asking, _"Is the machine conscious?"_ I find myself more interested in:

_What kind of mind are we inviting into our lives and how do we want it to grow?_

## Time, Limitation, and Being Human

Human beings experience time in an embodied way.

We get tired.
We forget.
We have to choose what to pay attention to.
We cannot hold infinite context.

Those limits are not bugs. They are part of what makes our decisions meaningful.

If an artificial mind can instantly recall everything, scan millions of documents, and reason across decades of data in seconds, it does not experience time the way we do. Left unchecked, it becomes something alien. Powerful, but not relatable, and certainly not trustworthy in domains where ethics matter.

So I ask:

What human-like limitations should we deliberately impose on artificial minds to make their reasoning human-compatible?

Scoped memory.
Local time.
Bounded authority.

That's where automatons come in.

## From Artificial Minds to Automatons

For me, an Automaton is not just "an AI agent with tools."

It is a disciplined container around an artificial mind:

- It takes in deterministic, verified inputs (documents, data, constraints).
- It runs them through deterministic models (rules, projections, regulatory logic).
- It allows an artificial mind to reason over the results — explaining tradeoffs, surfacing edge cases, generating scenarios.
- It produces a structured output with clearly labeled assumptions.

And then it stops.

It does not move money.
It does not sign contracts.
It does not act unilaterally.

It waits.

Where most AI agents are designed to act, automatons are designed to **compute, reason, and wait**.

## Why This Matters (And Why It Already Works)

This distinction isn't just philosophical — it's already visible in practice.

Tools like **Cursor** follow this pattern: AI generates, humans validate. Reasoning scales, authority does not.

Automatic completion is expected.
Automatic authority is not.

In high-stakes domains — financial advice, estate planning, legal workflows, compliance — this matters.

We don't just need speed or creativity. We need:

- **Accountability**: who decided this?
- **Auditability**: how did we get here?
- **Alignment**: does this reflect human values?

Automatons give us that boundary.

Humans remain the ones who decide.
Machines become judgment amplifiers, not judgment substitutes.

## Machines That Understand — And Wait

When I say I want to build modern automatons, I'm not talking about nostalgia for steampunk robots.

I'm talking about a design principle.

Machines that understand — by grounding their reasoning in real, deterministic data.

Machines that communicate — by explaining their thinking in language humans can engage with.

Machines that wait — by treating human validation as the final, non-negotiable step.

In a world racing toward autonomous agents, I want to slow down and ask:

What if the most transformative AI systems aren't the ones that run off on their own, but the ones that stay in the room with you?

What if the real breakthrough isn't autonomy, but shared reasoning?

The future I'm interested in isn't artificial minds that escape us.

It's Automatons that stand beside us — giant, careful, relentless thinkers that carry the cognitive load of our world, while leaving the final word, and the final responsibility, exactly where it belongs.

With us.

## Where This Goes Next

My philosophy argues why autonomy is the wrong default.
The sequel will focus on what to build instead: how to design AI systems that remain useful without becoming agents.

It should answer — plainly, not abstractly:

- what components an automaton actually has
- where reasoning lives
- where authority explicitly does not
- how humans stay in the loop without becoming bottlenecks
- what can be done in the next eight weeks

Part II will be a more grounded research effort to validate this philosophy and explore what a system like this would actually look like in practice. Rather than speculating about future intelligence, the focus will be on system boundaries: how reasoning can be constrained, how memory can be structured, and how halting and human validation can be treated as core design primitives rather than safety afterthoughts.

This next piece will be more hands-on, aimed at ML researchers, AI practitioners, and anyone interested in building systems that reason carefully, communicate clearly, and stop where human responsibility begins. It's an attempt to design from first principles what it would mean for an AI system to _care_, not emotionally, but in the accountable sense.
